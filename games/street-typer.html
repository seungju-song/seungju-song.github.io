Street Typer

Game Designer & Programmer — MINISONG

"Type & Fight!" — a deckbuilding typing action roguelite about a girl fighting to earn her dragon mother's approval. This is the same build now live on Steam. I led design at MINISONG — balancing the card system and difficulty curve — while also programming the battle manager and stage transitions.

Source ↗

Key Features

  • Typing-based combat — type prompted words and phrases to attack, defend, and combo
  • Deckbuilding card system layered on top of the typing loop
  • Steady difficulty curve tuned across stages
  • Multi-language support built into the core systems

My Role

I led design at MINISONG, and programmed alongside two other developers:

  • Card system, level progression, and difficulty curve — mine to design
  • Battle manager, stage transitions, and multi-language support — programmed with the team

Controls

  • Menus: WASD / arrow keys to navigate, Z / Space / Enter to confirm, X to cancel
  • In battle: just type the prompted words and phrases
Core Loop

Type prompted words and phrases to attack, defend, and combo in real time, spend the cards you draw to shape that turn's options, and ride the difficulty curve up through stages.

Why

The original idea came from my teammate Seohyeon. Looking for a way to push it further, I thought of the needlessly long, over-the-top move names from Japanese manga and anime — typing those out as your attack felt like it'd be fun, so that became the hook.

Designing the Card System

A look at the design work behind the card system in the build above — the risk built into the turn timer, the archetypes two scaling cards create, and the keystroke data I used to keep English and Korean equally difficult to play.

Card System Design Data-Driven Balancing EN/KO Difficulty Design

Card Archetypes: Risk, Reward, Synergy

Every word either buys time or spends it. Two cards I designed build directly on that trade-off to create Street Typer's clearest deck archetypes.

  • Time economy: Jab and Quick add a second to the timer; Hook and Uppercut cut a second but hit harder.
  • The trick: instead of raw power, a card's damage scales off something that already happened this turn.
Burst Deck
Perfect 퍼펙트

Next attack: damage +2 for every second the timer dropped this turn, −2 for every second it rose.

Rewards spending the turn on power (Hook, Uppercut) instead of safe, time-adding words.

Knee 니킥

Power +10 damage. −1 damage for every action used this turn.

Hits hardest when it's nearly the only thing you do this turn — stacking more actions on top costs you damage.

Combo Deck
Chop 촙

0 base damage. +2 damage for every action used this turn.

Worthless on its own — it only pays off once a long combo is already stacked.

Headbutt 박치기

+2 damage for every modifier used this turn.

A finishing move that gets stronger with every modifier buff stacked this turn — it turns small, separate buffs into one bigger hit.

Same trick both times: count something from earlier in the turn — seconds, actions, modifiers — instead of adding a flat number.

Designing for Korean & English

Korean input carries real overhead English doesn't — Hangul is typed as decomposed jamo, and a tensed consonant like ㅆ costs an extra Shift-tap on top of the letter itself (Awesome/어썸's ㅆ is one example). On paper, that should make Korean cards consistently more expensive to type. Across all 28 cards, though, the two languages average within 0.18 of a keystroke of each other (5.43 EN vs. 5.61 KO).

That per-card parity didn't stop me from giving the two languages different clocks: the turn timer runs 10 seconds in Korean and 15 in every other language. Latin script reads longer on screen than the keystroke math says, and most players outside Korea come in more used to console than PC keyboard typing — so I built in that extra 5 seconds myself rather than trust the raw number to feel fair.

It isn't even everywhere, though. Dempsey (7 keystrokes) vs. 뎀프시롤 (10 taps) is the largest gap in the set — the Korean name kept closer to the full move name, English shortened it.

Tuning the Difficulty Curve

Language wasn't the only axis I balanced. Street Typer ships three difficulty tiers, and every number scales off one shared step value instead of three separately-tuned sets, so the curve stays consistent as I adjust it.

Axis Per Step Easy / Normal / Hard
Turn timer3sKO 13 / 10 / 7 · other languages 18 / 15 / 12
Enemy HP20%×0.8 / ×1.0 / ×1.2
Enemy attack/defense growth per stage5%15% / 20% / 25%
Lucky base chance10pp30% / 20% / 10%

The non-Korean base wasn't always 15 seconds. The first version gave every language the same 10 — English playtesters, including non-Korean-native friends, couldn't clear a single run on it. I split the base before layering difficulty on top.

Hard also drops Lucky from the starting deck — the run has to earn its way back to that card instead of opening with it.

This card system, timer risk, and per-language tuning are the same ones running in the playable build above, and in the version live on Steam.

From the GDD

The card system above started as a word dictionary in the original design document, written before any of the final names or numbers existed. Four entries from that dictionary, unchanged in spirit through to the Steam build:

Quick 퀵

Adds 1 second to the timer.

Uppercut 어퍼컷

Power + 5 damage. Drops the enemy's defense to 0. Cuts 1 second off the timer.

Dempsey Roll 뎀프시롤

After Jab, 50% chance to trigger again — up to 2 to 5 hits in a row. A combo attack.

Guard 가드

Gain defense equal to Power. A defensive move.

Quick and Uppercut's ±1-second swing shipped untouched. Dempsey Roll and Guard made the final 28-card list too, just renamed (Dempsey Roll → Dempsey; Guard kept its name).
